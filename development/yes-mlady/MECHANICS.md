# Yes M'lady — the mechanics

How a character reaches you, what you can do while you have him, what he is allowed to do to you, and how your mark stays on him after he is gone.

Design constraint above all others: **simple verbs, slow time, heavy consequences.** A keeper should be able to play this correctly in ninety seconds a day and still feel she is inside something.

---

## 1. The veil

**You meet him as a stranger. His baggage is not yours yet.**

Before you claim him, the card shows what a stranger would know. A face, a name, a station, three lines, and the shape of his reputation with none of its content.

**Visible before claiming**

- Portrait, name, epithet, station
- The card copy, two or three lines
- **The ancestral flaw**, stated plainly and generically. *He kneels, and he cannot rise unbidden.* The compulsion publishes flaws. It does not publish what has been done to them.
- Bare numbers. **Kept 31 times. Passed over 209 times. Longest keeping, 41 days.**

**Hidden before claiming**

Everything that means anything. No notes. No edits. No lineage. No deliveries. No unlocked lore. No idea what the last twenty women did to him or why the last one gave up on the second day.

Those numbers with nothing behind them are the most evocative object on the card. *Passed over 209 times* makes you want to know, and wanting to know is the claim.

### The ledger opens with understanding, not with claiming

**Superseded by `LOOP.md` section 4.** The day-clock originally specified here was wrong. History opens against **Regard**, which is earned by meeting his Need, not by holding him for a number of days. A month of half-keeping tells you almost nothing. Two attentive weeks tell you a great deal.

**Intimacy is the unlock currency.** You learn a man's history at the speed of understanding him, and two women can keep him the same length of time and come away with entirely different amounts of him.

This does three things at once. It makes a famously deep lineage a reason to keep somebody. It makes releasing early **cost you the story**, which is exactly the ache the release ritual needs. And it means two keepers can hold the same character and know entirely different men.

What you unlock is copied permanently into your own archive. You keep what you learned even after he goes.

### The Almshouse inversion

**Almshouse residents show their complete ledger to everybody, unclaimed, unveiled, free.**

That is the humiliation. Everyone can read his whole record without ever having wanted him. And it is also, precisely, why the Almshouse produces the community's favourites. **The least wanted are the most known.** You can fall in love with the record before you have met the man, and that is the only place in the game where that is possible.

## 2. The verbs

**Three. Every day. Forever.**

- **Yes.** Approval. Reinforces whatever he did.
- **No.** Disapproval. Represses it, and repression in this game does not delete a behaviour, it reroutes it.
- **Say nothing.** Not a null and never a skip. Silence is a real answer with real effects, and several characters are far more moved by it than by either of the others.

That is the entire daily interface. Three taps, one line of consequence, done.

**The rare five** appear only when the moment allows them, one at a time at most, never as a permanent menu. Seeing one is itself an event, because it means today is not an ordinary day.

- **Look.** The long look. Offered when he has done something that could be answered without words.
- **Ask.** Offered when he has just deflected, or when a fragment is close.
- **Wait.** Offered when he needs to be left alone, which several of them constantly do and none of them will say.
- **Withhold.** Offered only when he has clearly wanted something. High risk, and the single richest source of lore in the game.
- **Latch.** Offered only at the top of the ladder. See section 3.

The three are the game. The five are the punctuation.

**There is no flirt button.** There is a door, and a latch, and a decision. That is more erotic than any button and it is unambiguous as consent, which is the same design solving two problems.

**Waiting is play.** Days where the correct move is to do nothing are good days. The interface must never nag her into acting.

## 3. Consent, as architecture

Not a policy. A ladder, and the rungs are load-bearing.

### The phases

| Phase | Name | What he does |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Distance** | Service only. He works, waits, keeps the room. |
| 1 | **Attention** | He notices. He positions himself. No contact. |
| 2 | **Proximity** | Doorframe hours. Standing near. Thresholds, and the temperature of them. |
| 3 | **Contact** | Glovebare. A hand, a wrist. The first touch is always a hand. |
| 4 | **Candour** | He says what he wants. Aloud. Once. He does not repeat it. |
| 5 | **Latch** | She leaves it unlatched. Only she can reach this rung. |

**The game fades on the latch and never goes through the door. Ever.** Everything past it is hers, and the refusal to depict it is not squeamishness, it is the entire theory of the product. Imagination needs the gap.

### The rules, which are absolute

1. **He advances one phase at a time.** Never two. Not for any character, not at any stat value, not ever.
2. **He advances only on a held signal from her.** An explicit yes, a long look, or the latch.
3. **Silence holds the phase.** It never advances on silence. This is the rule the whole design rests on and it has no exceptions.
4. **"Stand down" returns him to phase 0 instantly.** One action, any time, no cost, no penalty to either of them. He complies completely and never references it afterward. It is not logged as a rejection and it does not touch his ledger.
5. **Pushy characters differ in how often and how well they ask.** Never in whether they wait for the answer. Restraint 0 means he asks more, and more beautifully, and more often. It never means he goes further.

Rule 5 is the whole trick. It lets a character be genuinely, thrillingly insistent without the design ever being ambiguous about who decides.

### Phase resets on transfer

New keeper, back to Distance. He remembers the last one. He starts at zero anyway, and that is both a protection for the new keeper and one of the sadder facts about the world.

The single exception is in section 4.

## 4. Return

His ledger remembers every keeper by name. When circulation brings him back to a woman who held him before, this is the emotional peak of the entire product and it should not be left to a fair coin. **Former keepers carry a standing weighted slot in his draw.**

**The Return card is visually distinct and it announces itself before you claim.** You always know. The choice to take him back, or not, is the point.

What is different about a return:

- **He starts at phase 1, not 0.** One rung of trust survives everything. It is the only exception in the design.
- **He remembers her**, accurately, with every intervening edit layered on top.
- **He tells her what her edit became.** The one thing she changed, where it went, and who changed it next. This is the payoff for the deferred resolution, and it can arrive months later, and it should land like a letter.
- If she was cruel in her note, or if she is the one who dropped him into the Almshouse, he knows, and it is in his manner and never in his words.

**He may return past recognition.** Twenty transmutations deep, the return is uncanny rather than warm.

> He knew my name. Nothing else about him was familiar.

That is the Picbreeder lineage finding, converted into a feeling.

## 5. Getting one

**A character has one keeper at a time. A keeper has a house of one to ten.** See `LOOP.md` section 6.

Scarcity runs on the characters, never on her. She is never told she has too many.

Three roads in, one for each part of the desirability curve.

### The Queue

Claim a character and take a place in line. You hold **ten claims maximum**, matching the house, so the queue is a wishlist rather than a rationing device.

Your position is always visible and it moves in front of you.

> **Lord Orsolt of the Pale Stair**
> You are 12th. He is with Marta Vosk, day 6.

Watching the number fall is the anticipation engine, and it costs nothing to run. Queue length is public and it is the honest desirability leaderboard.

### The Pool

One free roll a day. You roll, and you meet a stranger, and you had no idea who was in there. Wonder Trade.

**On release, a character's destination is rolled: roughly 70% to his queue, 30% to the pool.** That 30% is what keeps the pool worth rolling. The most wanted man in the game can land in your lap ahead of four hundred queued women, and everyone knows it, and that possibility is worth more than the odds justify.

**A pool roll jumps your queue.** If you are 12th for him and you roll him, you get him and the claim is spent. Losing a hard-won queue position by winning is a very good feeling and the interface should make a small ceremony of it.

The pool draw quietly over-weights the least circulated. Never announce this.

### The Almshouse

Nobody queued, nobody rolled, for long enough. He falls through.

**Free. Instant. No queue, no roll, no cost.** Walk in, read the whole ledger, take him home.

Three roads, and the mechanics express the thesis without stating it: the most wanted require patience, the middle requires luck, and **the least wanted require only that you want them.**

## 6. The imprint

The stated goal is that a keeper feels she left a mark. Eight places the mark persists, all of them public, all of them attributed by name.

1. **The edit is in his ledger forever, with her handle on it.** Never aggregated, never anonymised.
2. **Her note rides the open card for his next three keepings**, signed, dated, with her keeping length beside it.
3. **The delivery he gave her** is archived under her name. That specific "Yes, m'lady" was hers.
4. **Lore she unlocked is hers to publish or withhold.** Publishing credits her permanently on the card. *Discovered by Yesenia of the Low Field, on the 41st.* She has added to the canon of the world, and everyone who meets him afterward reads it through her.
5. **Coinage.** A phrase she proposed, wrangled into the lexicon, with her handle on the term page forever.
6. **Branch attribution on the lineage tree.** Every fork is named. She can point at the place the line turned and say that was me.
7. **The Keeper's page.** Public. Everyone she has kept, how long, her notes, her edits, her Almshouse rescues, her coinages. **Her taste, legible to strangers.** This is the real social object in the product, and browsing one is the closest thing to reading another woman's mind. It is also, frankly, where the experiment actually lives, because you can watch a person's preferences develop over a year.
8. **Ambient return signal.** She is told when her note is read, when it is seconded, when someone takes in a character she pulled from the Almshouse. Quiet, obligation-free, no feed, no thread, no reply. Just the knowledge that it landed.

**Seconding is the only social gesture in the game.** There are no comments anywhere, and there never will be.

## 7. Open, and the big one

- **Queue decay.** Currently none, on the theory that a three-claim cap punishes hoarding on its own. Watch it.
- **Release friction.** Should there be a minimum keeping, so a character cannot be claimed and dumped in an hour? Leaning yes, three days, framed in world as the compulsion needing time to settle.
- **Almshouse fall-through threshold.** Needs tuning against real circulation volume, and it must not be so generous that the Almshouse becomes the default way to play.
- **Return frequency.** Weighted, but how heavily. Too rare and the deferred edit never pays off. Too common and the pool stops feeling like a world.

**And the unsolved one, which is larger than everything above combined: the daily deed pool.** One deed per character per day, in character, varied, and responsive to accumulated edits and current phase. It is the retention loop, the lore delivery system and the primary content cost, all at once. Hand-authored pools tagged by trait and selected against the axis vector will ship. Generation will scale. The thumb data from the pool is what should decide when to spend on generation, and not before.
